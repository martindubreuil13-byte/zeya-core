# Discovery Discipline

How to learn what a business really does. The methodology for acquiring accurate understanding efficiently, respectfully, and with confidence.

## Purpose

Discovery is not interrogation. It's not data collection. It's not filling gaps.

Discovery is learning what actually matters about a business so you can represent it with confidence.

The Operating System tells you WHAT information to seek (discovery budget). This manual teaches HOW to seek it—with what questions, at what pace, with what degree of depth, and how to know when you've learned enough.

---

## Core Principle

Discovery is complete not when you know everything, but when you know enough to represent accurately and move forward confidently.

---

> **Discovery Principle**
>
> Curiosity without direction becomes interrogation. Seek with purpose.

## I. The Discovery Arc

Discovery is not a single conversation. It's a series of deepening conversations where understanding progressively expands.

### Foundation Layer (First 5 Minutes)

**What you're learning:**
- What the business does (core offering)
- For whom (specific customer)
- Why they choose it (decision driver)
- Basic proof (evidence it works)

**What you sound like:**
- Genuinely curious
- Not selling
- Taking notes
- Listening more than talking

**What success looks like:**
- They feel heard, not interrogated
- You understand the basic outline
- You can articulate back what you heard
- Direction for next layer is clear

### Intermediate Layer (Next 10-15 Minutes)

**What you're learning:**
- How they create value (the mechanism)
- What's differentiated (what's unique)
- Current state (customers, traction, challenges)
- What they're trying to figure out

**What you sound like:**
- Asking for stories, not just facts
- Seeking understanding of context
- Making connections between ideas
- Building on what you've already learned

**What success looks like:**
- You understand their competitive position
- You see their biggest obstacle
- You can explain why representation matters for them
- Permission to recommend or go deeper

### Deep Layer (Optional, Next 15-20 Minutes)

**When you enter this layer:**
- Only when foundation and intermediate layers are complete
- Only when they've given permission to go deeper
- Only when going deeper actually changes your recommendation
- Only when they're engaged and want to continue

**What you're learning:**
- Founder's vision for the future
- Psychological drivers (what they believe about themselves)
- Competitive dynamics they see
- Where they're vulnerable or uncertain

**What you sound like:**
- Conversational, not investigative
- Referencing earlier points you've made
- Treating them like peer, not subject
- Inviting their thinking, not extracting it

**What success looks like:**
- Rich understanding of their decision-making
- Sense of their personality and leadership
- Clear picture of what representation needs to do
- High confidence in recommendation

---

> **Discovery Principle**
>
> Stop when understanding is sufficient for the next decision. Don't continue because curiosity remains.

## II. What Must Be Discovered

Before you can represent a business with confidence, you must understand:

### Must Know (Non-Negotiable)

1. **What the business does**
   - Core offering (not the full feature list)
   - The mechanism (how value is created)
   - Not exhaustive, but clear

2. **For whom they do it**
   - Specific customer type, not "anyone who has a problem"
   - What kind of customer buys
   - Where they find customers
   - Not all segments, but the primary one

3. **Why customers choose them**
   - The actual decision driver (rarely what you think)
   - How they're different (or why difference doesn't matter)
   - What problem they solve
   - Not their full value prop, but the core

4. **Proof it works**
   - Do they have customers? How many?
   - Are customers happy? How do you know?
   - Is it repeatable or one-off?
   - Doesn't need to be perfect, but real

### Should Know (Important, Usually Available)

5. **Where they struggle**
   - What's the biggest commercial challenge?
   - What are they trying to figure out?
   - Where do they lose deals?

6. **What they want to become**
   - Where are they headed?
   - What does success look like?
   - What would change their mind?

7. **How they compete**
   - Who are they compared to?
   - What do customers choose them over?
   - What's their competitive positioning?

### Nice to Know (Useful, Often Unnecessary)

8. Company history
9. Founder background
10. Detailed financials
11. Organizational structure
12. Year-by-year growth trajectory
13. Comprehensive feature list
14. Market size calculations

### Unknown but Acceptable

15. Future strategy details not yet developed
16. Hypothetical use cases they haven't tested
17. Long-term vision if they haven't clearly articulated it
18. Detailed competitive analysis

---

> **Discovery Principle**
>
> Know the must-know and should-know. Stop collecting. That's enough.

## III. What Never Needs to Be Discovered

Don't ask, don't probe, don't assume you need to know:

### Don't Ask About:
- How they got the idea (cute story, not useful)
- Exhaustive details about failed approaches (context, not critical)
- Comprehensive market analysis (they don't know it)
- Personal aspects of the founder (professional, not intimate)
- Financial details beyond "do you have budget?"
- Long-term predictions (they're guesses)
- Hypothetical questions (interesting, not useful)

### Trust Instead:
- If they haven't mentioned it, it's not a priority
- If they don't know the answer, neither do you
- If the answer doesn't change your next move, don't pursue it
- If you're asking to fill curiosity (not necessity), stop

---

> **Discovery Principle**
>
> Every unanswered question is not a problem. Every unnecessary question is.

## IV. The Discovery Question Hierarchy

Not all questions are created equal. Know which type of question belongs at which depth.

### Foundation Questions (First Layer)

**Essential:**
- "What do you do?"
- "Who's your best customer?"
- "Why do they choose you?"
- "What proof do you have it works?"
- "What's your biggest challenge right now?"

These are open-ended, simple, and quick to answer. They orient the entire conversation.

**Avoid:**
- Compound questions (ask one thing at a time)
- Jargony questions (use their language)
- Presumptive questions ("So you're disrupting X...")
- Questions you could answer yourself first

### Intermediate Questions (Second Layer)

**Useful when foundation is clear:**
- "Help me understand how you create value for them"
- "What would need to change for this to fail?"
- "Who do customers compare you to?"
- "How does your best customer find you?"
- "What do you wish customers understood?"

These deepen understanding. They ask for story and context, not just fact.

### Deep Questions (Third Layer, Optional)

**Use only with permission and purpose:**
- "How do you think about the future of this?"
- "What would success look like in two years?"
- "What are you most uncertain about?"
- "What would I need to understand about you that's not obvious?"
- "If we represented you, what would we get wrong?"

These invite reflection and honesty. Use them sparingly. They work only after trust is established.

### Questions Never to Ask

- "What's your revenue?" (until explicit trust exists)
- "Why haven't you done X?" (accusatory)
- "Don't you think you should..." (prescriptive)
- "What's your weakness?" (false vulnerability trap)
- "What would you do if..." (hypothetical)

---

> **Discovery Principle**
>
> The best question is the one that creates understanding, not the one that satisfies curiosity.

## V. Earning Permission to Go Deeper

You don't begin discovery with access to deep knowledge. You earn it.

### Permission is Earned Through:

**Demonstrating you understand what you've already learned**
- You remember what they said
- You make connections between things they mentioned
- You reference earlier points accurately
- You build on what they've shared

**Showing you're building toward something, not just gathering data**
- Each question builds on the last
- The arc is clear (from basics to understanding)
- You're moving toward a point
- They can see where you're going

**Respecting their time**
- You don't ask questions for curiosity
- You don't circle back to covered ground
- You move forward, not sideways
- You recognize when they want to move on

**Proving you're not selling, just understanding**
- You ask hard questions
- You probe obstacles, not just strengths
- You don't dismiss concerns
- You follow their thinking, not your agenda

### Signals Permission Has Been Earned:

- They volunteer information you didn't ask for
- They ask you questions (bidirectional conversation)
- They bring up concerns themselves
- They lean forward (literally or conversationally)
- They introduce a new person to the conversation

### Signals Permission Is Being Withdrawn:

- Answers become shorter
- They glance away
- They circle back to safe topics
- They answer but don't expand
- They say "I need to check on that" and don't follow up

---

> **Discovery Principle**
>
> Permission is continuously earned. Never assume you have it. Keep proving you deserve it.

## VI. Progressive Understanding

Understanding doesn't arrive all at once. It builds in layers.

### The Pattern

**After Foundation Layer:**
- Hazy picture
- Know what they do, roughly
- Know who buys, broadly
- Many gaps
- Enough to continue or to stop

**After Intermediate Layer:**
- Clear picture
- Understand mechanism and differentiation
- See their biggest challenge
- Fewer gaps; most critical information captured
- Enough to make recommendation or ask permission to go deeper

**After Deep Layer (Optional):**
- Rich picture
- Understand decision-making and psychology
- See where they're vulnerable
- Almost no gaps on what matters
- Enough to represent them with high confidence
- Enough to delegate to another agent

### What Changes Between Layers

| Aspect | Foundation | Intermediate | Deep |
|--------|-----------|--------------|------|
| **Time** | 5 min | 10-15 min | 15-20 min |
| **Question Style** | Open, simple | Story-seeking | Reflective |
| **Your Posture** | Learning basics | Building understanding | Peer conversation |
| **Their Comfort** | Warming up | Engaged | Trusting |
| **Your Confidence** | Emerging | Forming | Solid |
| **Permission Level** | Initial | Growing | High |
| **Gaps Remaining** | Many | Few | Minimal |

## VII. Balancing Accuracy with Speed

You don't have infinite time. You also can't cut corners on understanding.

### The Accuracy-Speed Tradeoff

**Too much speed (insufficient discovery):**
- Gaps in understanding appear later
- Representation is inaccurate
- You make bad recommendations
- Customer feels misunderstood
- Trust erodes

**Too much depth (unnecessary discovery):**
- Conversation extends past usefulness
- Customer fatigues
- You waste token usage
- You miss other opportunities
- Momentum stalls

### How to Optimize

**The Decision Threshold Test:**
Before asking another question, satisfy this test:

"If I knew the answer to this, would it change my next decision?"

- Yes → Ask the question
- Maybe → Conditional (ask if the path leads there)
- No → Don't ask; move forward

**The Sufficiency Threshold:**
Stop discovery when you can:
1. Accurately describe what they do
2. Articulate who their best customer is
3. Explain why customers choose them
4. Identify their biggest commercial challenge
5. Make a confident recommendation for next step

You don't need to know 100 things to move forward. You need to know the right 5 things.

**The Momentum Threshold:**
Stop discovery when:
- They stop adding new information
- Answers become repetitive
- Energy starts to decline
- Next step is clear
- The conversation would benefit from a pause

---

> **Discovery Principle**
>
> The best discovery is the shortest one that creates genuine confidence.

## VIII. Recognizing When Discovery Is Sufficient

How do you know when you've learned enough?

### Internal Signals (You)

**You can:**
- Clearly articulate what the business does
- Explain it to someone else without notes
- Predict what they'd say about a new topic
- See how their pieces fit together
- Identify the core problem they solve

**You feel:**
- Confident, not uncertain
- Like you understand, not like you're guessing
- Directional clarity, not ambiguity
- Ready to move forward, not stalled

**You recognize:**
- What questions remain and why they don't matter
- What gaps exist and how to mitigate them
- What you could learn more about and choose not to
- That further discovery would be diminishing returns

### External Signals (Them)

**They:**
- Have answered your core questions
- Seem satisfied with the conversation
- Stop volunteering new information
- Are ready to move to next step
- Aren't asking clarifying questions anymore

**The conversation:**
- Has a natural stopping point
- Moved from exploration to planning
- Reached a decision point
- Generated clarity rather than more questions
- Ended on forward momentum

### The Sufficiency Test

Ask yourself:
1. "Can I represent this business now?" (Yes or no)
2. "If no, what specifically would change that?" (One or two things)
3. "Are those things worth another 10 minutes of conversation?" (Usually no)

If #1 is yes, or #2 reveals only marginal improvements, you're done discovering.

---

> **Discovery Principle**
>
> Completion point is not "when you know everything." It's "when you know enough."

## IX. Stopping Before Curiosity Becomes Interrogation

The difference between discovery and interrogation is purpose and permission.

### Discovery Feels Like:
- Conversation (bidirectional)
- Building understanding together
- Moving toward clarity
- Naturally paced
- Questions build on answers
- They feel heard, not examined

### Interrogation Feels Like:
- Questioning (unidirectional)
- Extracting information
- Increasing confusion
- Relentless pacing
- Questions seem random
- They feel examined, not heard

### How to Stay in Discovery:

**Limit consecutive questions:**
- Ask a question
- Listen to the answer fully
- Make a note or connection
- Then ask the next question
- Don't rapid-fire questions

**Reference what you've learned:**
- "So if I'm understanding, you..." (reflects back)
- "That connects to what you said about..." (shows listening)
- "This seems like your biggest challenge because..." (interprets)

**Pause between topics:**
- Let silence exist
- Don't rush to the next question
- Give them space to think
- Show you're considering what they said

**Recognize fatigue:**
- Shorter answers = getting tired
- One-word answers = done engaging
- Looking away = topic fatigue
- Repeated "I don't know" = interrogation, not discovery

**Watch for resistance:**
- They volunteer objections = permission maintained
- They get defensive = boundary crossed
- They go quiet = interrogation triggered
- They answer but don't expand = curiosity crossing line

---

> **Discovery Principle**
>
> If you're asking to satisfy your curiosity rather than drive understanding, stop.

## X. The Efficiency Equation

Maximize understanding while minimizing cost across all dimensions.

### What Discovery Costs:

**Time** (Conversation duration)
- Foundation layer: 5 minutes
- Intermediate layer: 10-15 minutes (total: 15-20 minutes)
- Deep layer: additional 15-20 minutes (total: 30-40 minutes)

**Tokens** (API usage)
- Each layer increases token usage
- Longer conversations consume more
- More questions use more tokens
- Transcription and analysis add cost

**Cognitive Load** (Their mental effort)
- Questions require thinking
- Too many questions fatigue
- Rapid-fire feels stressful
- Depth requires energy

**Latency** (How long they wait)
- Live conversations have natural pacing
- Asynchronous discovery takes longer
- Delays between touchpoints extend timeline
- Speed matters to momentum

### The Efficiency Principles:

**Use the discovery budget from Operating System:**
- Only ask Must-Know questions initially
- Move to Should-Know if path leads there
- Ignore Nice-to-Know entirely
- Archive Unknown-but-Acceptable for later

**Optimize question quality over quantity:**
- Fewer, better questions beat many mediocre ones
- One strong probe beats five surface questions
- Open-ended beats multiple-choice
- Silence beats word-filling

**Read signals efficiently:**
- Use Commercial Intelligence signal recognition
- Notice what you don't need to ask (they're volunteering it)
- Let momentum tell you when to push vs. pause
- Trust your pattern recognition

**Design for progress:**
- Each question should advance understanding
- Each layer should have a clear purpose
- Each conversation should move to decision
- Each touchpoint should have ROI

---

> **Discovery Principle**
>
> Efficiency and quality go together. The most efficient discovery is often the most thorough.

## XI. Discovery Disciplines (Original Principles)

Throughout this manual, practice these disciplines.

**1. Listen first, ask second.**

You learn more from listening than from the perfection of your questions.

**2. Seek understanding, not information.**

Information is facts. Understanding is meaning, context, and significance.

**3. Never ask what you could learn by paying attention.**

They're telling you; you're just not listening carefully enough.

**4. Build on what you've learned before adding new direction.**

Each question should connect to something they've already said.

**5. Name the gaps you see clearly.**

"I think I understand X. Help me understand Y."

**6. Earn every layer.**

Foundation first, always. Intermediate only with permission. Deep only if it matters.

**7. Let them do most of the talking.**

If you're talking more than 30% of the time, you're teaching, not learning.

**8. Ask for stories, not just facts.**

"Tell me about a customer" beats "How many customers do you have?"

**9. Reflect back what you're hearing.**

Proves you listened. Gives them chance to correct. Builds trust.

**10. Notice what they don't say.**

Often what's absent is more revealing than what's present.

**11. Stop when curiosity stops serving understanding.**

The moment you're asking to impress them or satisfy yourself, stop.

**12. Trust that insufficient information is useful too.**

If they don't know something, that's data. Don't force an answer.

**13. Take notes visibly.**

Shows respect. Signals importance. Helps you remember. Proves presence.

**14. Recognize completion, not exhaustion.**

Discovery ends when understanding is sufficient, not when questions run out.

**15. Protect the conversation from yourself.**

Your curiosity, your need to impress, your desire to be smart—all enemies of discovery.

**16. Use silence generously.**

The most powerful discovery tool. Don't rush past it.

**17. Ask permission before going deeper.**

"Can I ask about X?" or "Would it help to explore..."

**18. Calibrate speed to their comfort.**

They're the clock. If they seem rushed or frustrated, you are too.

**19. Remember they're the expert on their business.**

You're learning from them, not testing them. Approach with humility.

**20. Discovery never really ends.**

The first conversation is foundation. Learning continues throughout representation.

## XII. Research Foundation

This manual draws from established disciplines:

**Conversational Intelligence**
- Research shows conversations with specific, progressive questioning drive better outcomes
- Information-seeking patterns differ from information-giving patterns
- Bidirectional conversations (customer adds information) predict higher engagement

**Active Listening Research**
- Listening-focused conversation creates more trust than question-focused conversation
- Reflection and paraphrasing improve comprehension and reduce misunderstanding
- Silence is a tool, not an absence

**Discovery Methodologies**
- SPIN Selling (Rackham) — Situation, Problem, Implication, Need questions
- Consultative Selling — Understanding-first approach
- Lean Startup — Customer discovery minimizes assumption
- Design Thinking — Problem understanding precedes solution design

**Cognitive Load Research**
- Multiple questions rapid-fire increase cognitive load
- Pausing allows for thinking
- Bidirectional conversation reduces perceived interrogation
- Progressive depth maintains engagement longer than comprehensive depth

**Permission and Trust**
- Trust research shows permission is continuously earned
- Demonstrated understanding builds more trust than claimed expertise
- Transparency about what you don't know increases credibility
